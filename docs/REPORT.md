# Gaze Detection for Online Assessments and Driver Monitoring

**Extended technical report.** This document supersedes the numbers in the original course
submission ([`DLP_Report_Final.pdf`](DLP_Report_Final.pdf)) wherever the two disagree, and
explains why they disagree. Every figure quoted here is produced by a script in
[`../scripts/`](../scripts) and stored as JSON under [`../results/`](../results).

Environment for all measurements: Windows 11, Python 3.11.1, TensorFlow 2.19.0,
MediaPipe 0.10.21, OpenCV 4.11.0, AMD Ryzen 5 3600 (6 cores / 12 threads), CPU only.

---

## 1. Problem and framing

Given a single frame from a consumer webcam, decide whether the person is facing the screen
(`front`) or has turned away to the `left` or `right`. The two motivating applications are
online-assessment integrity and driver inattention monitoring; both need a decision per
frame, on commodity hardware, with no GPU.

Two framing decisions matter for interpreting everything below.

**This is head-direction classification, not gaze-point regression.** The labels describe
where the head is pointed. Eye-in-socket movement is measured separately (§5.3) and is a
much weaker signal. A person who keeps their head still and moves only their eyes will
usually be classified `front`.

**Labels are defined in image space.** `left` means the face is turned towards the left edge
of the frame, which for a non-mirrored capture is the subject's own right. This convention is
inherited from how the folders were labelled during capture; `scripts/run_video.py --mirror`
flips the input for anyone who wants subject-relative semantics.

## 2. Data

### 2.1 Our captures

400 photographs of **3 consenting subjects**, captured on 2 phones across 3 rooms:

| Group | Images | Device | Setting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subject A | 152 | Galaxy S22 | patterned wall, warm light |
| Subject B | 201 | Galaxy S22 | cream wall, warm light |
| Subject C | 47 | Google Pixel | plain wall, cool light |

Class balance is close to uniform: 129 `front`, 142 `left`, 129 `right`. The committed split
is 319 / 40 / 41 (train / val / test), produced by a random 80-10-10 split over all images
using `train_test_split(random_state=42)`.

Images in this repository are downscaled to 512 px on the longest side, from originals of
roughly 2000×1500. §6 quantifies exactly what that costs each method.

### 2.2 Public datasets used but not redistributed

Used during earlier training stages; see [`../data/README.md`](../data/README.md) for links.

| Dataset | Role | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LFR face dataset (98 505 / 21 108 / 21 111) | EfficientNetB0 pre-training, 3-class | 73.7% on its own val split, 59.26% transferred to our photos |
| Kaggle Drowsy Detection | eye-state exploration | not in the final pipeline |
| Kaggle Open/Closed Eyes in the Wild | eye-state CNN | not in the final pipeline |
| Kaggle Eye Gaze (8-class eye regions) | eye-region gaze CNN | 46.0% when mapped to our 3 classes, near chance |

### 2.3 The split is optimistic by construction

A random split over 400 photographs from 3 subjects and 3 sessions puts near-duplicate
frames — same person, same room, same minute — on both sides of the boundary. Accuracy
measured this way answers "new photos of a known person". §7 measures the harder question.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Geometric head-yaw estimator (primary)

MediaPipe Face Mesh, with `refine_landmarks=True`, returns 478 landmarks. From three of
them we form one scale-invariant scalar:

```
yaw_score = ( d(nose, cheek_right) − d(nose, cheek_left) )
            / ( d(nose, cheek_right) + d(nose, cheek_left) )
```

using landmark 1 (nose tip) and 234 / 454 (cheek boundaries). Rotating the head towards one
side shortens one distance and lengthens the other; dividing by the sum removes dependence
on face size, camera distance and image resolution.

Classification is two thresholds, `low` and `high`, fitted by exhaustive search over pairs
drawn from 80 quantiles of the training-split scores:

```
yaw_score > high  →  left        yaw_score < low  →  right        otherwise  →  front
```

Fitted on the 319 training images: `low = −0.2356`, `high = +0.2496`. Two parameters in
total. Both are stored in [`../models/geometric_calibration.json`](../models/geometric_calibration.json)
alongside the accuracies they achieved, and are regenerated by
`python scripts/calibrate_geometric.py`.

The thresholds are asymmetric because the `front` cluster in our data is not centred on
zero — subjects tended to sit slightly off-axis from the camera. A single symmetric
magnitude threshold is measurably worse.

### 3.2 EfficientNetB0 (fallback)

ImageNet-initialised EfficientNetB0, 128×128 input, `GlobalAveragePooling2D` → `Dropout(0.3)`
→ `Dense(3, softmax)`, 4 053 414 parameters. Trained in two stages:

1. **Pre-training on LFR**, base frozen, 10 epochs Adam, then 6 epochs with the top layers
   unfrozen at learning rate 1e-5 with `EarlyStopping` and `ReduceLROnPlateau`. Reached
   73.7% val accuracy on LFR. Shipped as `head_dir_effnetb0_lfr.keras`.
2. **Fine-tuning on our 319 images**, augmented with flips, rotation, zoom, contrast,
   brightness, translation and Gaussian noise, using balanced class weights, Adam at 1e-4,
   `ModelCheckpoint` on val accuracy. Shipped as `head_dir_effnetb0_ft.keras`.

Inference must use whole frames, matching training. Cropping to the detected face costs 6.2
points (§5.2).

### 3.3 The cascade

```
Face Mesh landmarks available?  →  yes: geometric verdict
                                →  no:  EfficientNetB0 on the whole frame
                                →  no, and CNN disabled: 'unknown'
```

Face Mesh succeeds on 98.0% of our 400 images (96.3% of the 81 held-out ones). The CNN
covers the remainder: extreme profiles where the landmark model gives up but a whole-frame
classifier still has a chance. The pipeline never reports `front` when it simply failed to
find a face — `GazeResult.label` is `None` and `label_name` is `'unknown'`.

### 3.4 Video stabilisation

Per-frame predictions flicker near threshold boundaries. `MajorityVoteSmoother` votes over a
7-frame window and ignores undetermined frames, so one dropped detection cannot flip the
verdict; at 30 FPS the added lag is about 100 ms. `DwellTracker` raises an alert only after
gaze has stayed off-centre for a configurable number of seconds, which is what distinguishes
a normal glance from sustained inattention.

## 4. Protocol

- Thresholds are fitted on the **train** split only. The 81 val + test images are never seen
  by any fitting procedure, so geometric and CNN numbers are directly comparable.
- 5-fold stratified cross-validation over all 400 images (`random_state=42`) gives a tighter
  estimate than 81 images alone, refitting thresholds inside each fold.
- Leave-one-subject-out (§7) is the generalisation protocol.
- Latency uses 120 timed frames after 15 warm-up frames, reporting median and p95 rather
  than mean, because a mean hides the occasional slow frame that a real-time system cares
  about. Real photographs are used so Face Mesh has actual faces to find.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Main comparison

81 held-out images (`val` + `test`), from `results/metrics.json`:

| Method | Params | Accuracy | Macro F1 | Latency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cascade (geometric + CNN fallback) | 2 + 4.05 M | **96.30%** | 0.963 | 5.9 ms |
| Geometric head-yaw | 2 | 95.06% | 0.951 | 5.9 ms |
| EfficientNetB0 fine-tuned | 4.05 M | 80.25% | 0.794 | 78.5 ms |
| Geometric iris offset | 2 | 62.96% | 0.621 | 5.9 ms |
| EfficientNetB0 LFR only | 4.05 M | 59.26% | 0.549 | 78.5 ms |
| Majority class | 0 | 35.80% | 0.176 | — |

Per-class for geometric head-yaw — precision / recall: `front` 0.867 / 1.000,
`left` 1.000 / 0.931, `right` 1.000 / 0.923. Every error is a turn reported as `front`;
there is no left/right confusion at all.

Two qualifications on this table, both of which matter more than the ranking:

- **The cascade's lead over geometry alone is one frame.** 96.30% versus 95.06% on 81 images
  is 78 versus 77 correct. At this sample size one frame is 1.2 percentage points, so the
  two are indistinguishable. The cascade's real justification is not accuracy but coverage:
  it returns an answer on frames where Face Mesh fails instead of returning `unknown`.
- **3 of the geometric method's 4 errors are detection failures, not scoring failures.**
  Face Mesh finds nothing on 3 of the 81 images; those score 0.0, which falls inside the
  `front` band. Restricted to the 78 images where a face is found, the geometric estimator
  is **98.72% accurate (77/78)**, with a single genuine misclassification (§8).

5-fold cross-validation over all 400 images:

| Method | Mean ± std | Out-of-fold | Threshold stability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Geometric head-yaw | **94.75% ± 1.66%** | 94.75% | low −0.320 ± 0.031, high +0.271 ± 0.028 |
| Geometric iris offset | 55.50% ± 6.05% | 57.25% | low −0.033 ± 0.001, high +0.041 ± 0.003 |

Fold-to-fold threshold variation is small relative to the gap between class modes, which is
why the method is stable across resplits.

### 5.3 Auxiliary signals

**Iris offset.** Mean horizontal displacement of each iris centre from the midpoint of its
eye corners, normalised by eye width, sign-matched to the yaw score. 62.96% held out,
55.50% ± 6.05% under cross-validation. Genuine signal, far weaker than head rotation.

**Eye-aspect-ratio.** Standard six-point EAR per eye, averaged. Open-eye mean 0.337; the
closed threshold is set to the 1st percentile of the observed open-eye distribution (0.083).
**This is a heuristic, not a benchmarked classifier** — the local data contains no labelled
closed-eye images, so no accuracy is claimed.

### 5.4 Latency

`results/benchmark.json`, 640×480, CPU only:

| Stage | Median | p95 | Implied FPS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Face Mesh landmark detection | 5.62 ms | 18.74 ms | 178 |
| Geometric scoring + decision | 0.11 ms | 0.12 ms | 9381 |
| EfficientNetB0 inference | 71.66 ms | 75.49 ms | 14 |
| Full pipeline, geometric | 5.94 ms | 19.01 ms | **168** |
| Full pipeline, CNN every frame | 78.53 ms | 92.24 ms | 13 |

Measured over 400 timed frames after 40 warm-up frames. Run-to-run variation on a loaded
desktop is a few percent; the numbers above are the committed run.

End-to-end through `scripts/run_video.py` on a 300-frame 640×480 clip, including decode,
inference and overlay: 100 FPS geometric. The geometric decision itself is 1.8% of the frame
budget; Face Mesh is the cost.

## 6. Resolution sensitivity

Held-out accuracy as a function of the longest side of the input image
(`results/resolution_sweep.json`):

| Longest side | Detection rate | Geometric | CNN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 512 px | 96.3% | 95.06% | 80.25% |
| 384 px | 96.3% | 95.06% | 77.78% |
| 256 px | 96.3% | 95.06% | 72.84% |
| 192 px | 96.3% | 95.06% | 70.37% |
| 128 px | 96.3% | 95.06% | 62.96% |

The geometric estimator is flat across a 4× reduction; the CNN loses 17 points. Relative
landmark positions survive downsampling, and Face Mesh's own detector is robust at small
scales. Fine appearance detail does not survive, and that is what the CNN learned to use.

Measured against the original ~2000×1500 captures, the geometric estimator scores 96.30% and
the CNN 83.95% on the same held-out images (92.68% on the 41-image `test` split alone, which
is the figure the original course report quoted). Shipping 512 px images therefore costs the
CNN a few points and the geometric method nothing — a trade recorded here rather than hidden.

## 7. Generalisation to unseen subjects

`results/cross_subject.json`. Subjects are recovered from capture filenames; thresholds are
refitted from scratch on the other two subjects in each fold.

| Held-out subject | Train / test images | Fitted thresholds | Accuracy | Detection rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A (Galaxy S22, patterned wall) | 248 / 152 | −0.114 / +0.266 | 92.11% | 96.1% |
| B (Galaxy S22, cream wall) | 199 / 201 | −0.363 / +0.258 | 98.01% | 99.0% |
| C (Google Pixel, plain wall) | 353 / 47 | −0.246 / +0.360 | 72.34% | 100.0% |
| **Mean** | | | **87.49% ± 10.98%** | |

Three observations.

**The honest operating point is 87.5%, not 96.3%.** The 9-point gap is the cost of the
random split, and it is exactly the kind of gap that makes small-data results look better
than they are.

**Subject C, at 72.34%, independently reproduces the original report's own estimate** that
accuracy on genuinely new images was "up to 70%". Face Mesh detected that subject in 100% of
frames, so the failure is entirely in threshold transfer, not detection: the fitted `low`
for subject C's fold is −0.246 while the two Galaxy folds want −0.114 and −0.363. Camera
intrinsics and seating geometry shift where `front` sits on the scalar.

**This suggests the fix.** Because the whole model is two numbers, a few seconds of frontal
video per user would let the thresholds be calibrated per person — the cheapest possible
personalisation, and unavailable to a 4-million-parameter CNN.

The CNN is not evaluated under this protocol: it was fine-tuned on photographs of all three
subjects, so per-subject scores would not be generalisation estimates. Doing it properly
needs three re-trainings (§9).

## 8. Error analysis

Geometric head-yaw makes 4 errors in 81 held-out images, and they are not the same kind of
error:

| Image | True | Predicted | yaw | Face found |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `20250504_032701.jpg` | left | front | — | no |
| `20250504_032724.jpg` | left | front | — | no |
| `20250504_034642.jpg` | right | front | — | no |
| `PXL_20250504_184510210.jpg` | right | front | −0.097 | yes |

Three are **detection** failures — extreme profiles where Face Mesh returns nothing, so the
score defaults to 0.0 and lands in the `front` band. Only the last is a **scoring** failure:
a shallow turn at −0.097, comfortably inside the fitted band of [−0.236, +0.250]. It belongs
to subject C, the same subject that fails under leave-one-subject-out (§7).

So on frames where a face is found, the estimator is right 77 times out of 78. Its weakness
is mild rotations and its remaining exposure is the landmark detector, not the decision rule.
Both failure directions report `front` when the truth is a turn, which for proctoring is the
benign direction: the system misses a marginal glance rather than accusing someone who is
looking straight ahead.

EfficientNetB0 makes 16 errors on the same images with a qualitatively different structure:
7 of 26 `right` images are predicted `left`. Confusing the two directions is a semantic error
a geometric method structurally cannot make, and it is evidence the CNN never recovered the
geometry cleanly from 319 examples.

The cascade replaces those 3 undetected frames with CNN predictions. It fixes one, and gets
the other two wrong in a new way — introducing the left/right confusions that pure geometry
avoids. Net effect: one frame better, with a worse failure mode. That is the whole of the
1.24-point difference, and it is why the geometric estimator rather than the cascade is the
result this project stands behind.

## 9. Limitations and future work

Limitations are listed in the [README](../README.md#limitations); the short version is three
subjects, a 72.3% worst-case unseen subject, three coarse classes, head pose dominating over
eye movement, and an unbenchmarked blink signal.

The ordered list of things that would actually change the numbers:

1. **More subjects.** With 3 subjects, ±11% cross-subject spread is the dominant uncertainty.
   Nothing else on this list matters as much.
2. **Per-user threshold calibration.** §7 shows threshold transfer is the failure mode and
   the model is two scalars. Five seconds of frontal video should recover most of the gap.
3. **Leave-one-subject-out re-training of the CNN**, so both methods are measured under the
   harder protocol. Cheap: the fine-tuning stage is minutes of CPU time per fold.
4. **Continuous yaw regression** against a head-pose reference such as Hopenet, replacing
   three classes with an angle and thresholding downstream.
5. **A genuine eye-gaze branch.** The iris signal at 62.96% is the weakest link and is what
   stands between head-direction classification and actual gaze estimation.
6. **A labelled closed-eye split**, so the drowsiness component can be benchmarked instead
   of asserted.

## 10. What changed after submission

For anyone comparing this repository against the PDF:

- **Added the geometric estimator.** It was not in the original submission. It is now the
  primary path, and it beats the CNN we had trained by 15 points.
- **Corrected a class-order and colour-channel bug.** `notebooks/legacy/final_predictor.ipynb`
  declared `['left', 'right', 'front']` and fed BGR to a model trained on RGB with Keras's
  alphabetical `['front', 'left', 'right']`. It scored 12.20%, below chance. Corrected, the
  same weights reach 54.50%. The hand-tuned threshold rules in that notebook were
  compensating for the mislabelling.
- **Re-measured every headline number under a stated protocol.** The original 91% overall
  accuracy was not reproducible as stated. At original resolution the fine-tuned model
  reaches 92.68% on the 41-image `test` split but only 75.00% on `val`, and 83.95% across
  both; on the 512 px images committed here it reaches 80.25% across both. Quoting the best
  of two small splits is how 91% happened.
- **Replaced the ~25 FPS claim** with a per-stage breakdown: the geometric path is 168 FPS on
  CPU, the CNN path is 13 FPS. The original figure came from the CNN with 5-frame skipping.
- **Recorded negative results** for the custom CNN, the eye-region CNN and EfficientNetB3
  instead of omitting them.
- **Added leave-one-subject-out evaluation**, which is where the honest 87.5% comes from.

The original report's own caution — that accuracy on genuinely unseen images was closer to
70% — turned out to be the most accurate statement in it.
